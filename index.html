Why buyers get burned

Cocopeat goes wrong quietly.

Salt, screening and moisture are the three things that ruin a container of cocopeat, and not one of them is visible in a photograph. A sample can be perfect and the shipment still be wrong, because the sample was drawn from a different week's production.

That is the whole problem this business exists to solve, so we deal with it in the only way that actually works: everything that matters gets written down and agreed before anything is pressed, and the material is checked against that document before the container is sealed.

Nothing below is unusual. It is simply written down, which is more than most first orders get.

The material

A block is a shipping format. The medium only exists once it is wetted.

Compression is what makes cocopeat worth shipping, because you are moving the medium at a fraction of its working volume. Pick a state to see what that means in practice.

State 01 / as loaded

Dense, dry and stackable

This is the format that makes the freight economics work. It is also the state in which every quality decision has already been made and can no longer be changed, which is why the specification comes first.

State 02 / water added

Breaking down under water

Add water and the block gives way from the outside in. How cleanly it does that, and how much water it takes, follows directly from the compression ratio and the moisture at the press.

State 03 / ready to fill

Open, airy, holding water

This is what your grower is actually buying: a medium that holds water and air at the same time and behaves predictably under a drip line. The expansion figure for your batch comes off its analysis, not off this page.

Why one processor's pith is not another's

Coir pith is a by-product of husk processing, so it is only as consistent as the process it falls out of. Two units in the same district can turn out material that behaves differently under the same drip line, because of how long the pith sat in the yard, how well it was washed, how it was screened, and how wet it was when it went into the press. Choosing the unit matters more than choosing the country.

Before you write to us

The questions buyers actually ask.

What is your minimum?

A container is the usual first order. Smaller trial quantities can sometimes be arranged where it makes commercial sense. Tell us the volume you have in mind and we will be straight with you about it.

Can I see a sample first?

Yes, and it will be drawn from the production your order would come from rather than off a shelf. That is the only kind of sample worth judging a shipment by.

How long does it take?

It depends on the format, the quantity, and whether the material needs washing or buffering. Once the specification is settled you get a date rather than a range. Lead times stretch in the wet months, and we will say so rather than discover it later.

What if it does not match on arrival?

The agreed specification and the batch analysis are the reference, which is exactly why both exist before production. Send photographs and the analysis and we deal with it against that document, not against a memory of a conversation.

Do you supply washed, buffered or low-salt material?

All three are specified per order, to the crop. Buffering in particular is named to the crop rather than sold as a grade, because that is the only way it means anything.

Can you do private label?

Yes. Sleeves, cartons and shipping marks are printed to your artwork and your specification. Send the artwork with your enquiry and it gets priced in from the start.

Which Incoterms do you work on?

FOB, CFR or CIF. Tell us how you normally buy and we will quote on your terms rather than ours.

Why buy from a sourcing business rather than a factory?

Because no single unit is best at everything. Washed block, buffered slab and screened loose pith are not necessarily strongest at the same address, and a buyer tied to one plant gets whatever that plant is good at. We are not tied.

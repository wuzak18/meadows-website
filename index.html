01 Why cocopeat

Growers do not buy it to be green. They buy it because it behaves.

Coir pith is the fine, spongy fraction left when a coconut husk is worked for fibre. Three properties are why it took over from peat in high-value crops.

Water and air at once

Screened pith holds a large volume of water while keeping air around the roots, so a crop can be irrigated often without drowning. That combination is the whole reason it displaced peat in glasshouse production.

Predictable under a drip line

It re-wets evenly rather than channelling, and it drains consistently. A feed programme behaves the same in the last month of a crop as it did in the first, which is what makes it plannable.

Near-inert, so you stay in control

Washed pith carries very little nutrition of its own. What the crop receives is what you feed it, which is exactly what a grower working to a recipe wants.

Against peat

  • A by-product of a crop already being harvested, rather than a bog that took thousands of years to form.
  • Buyers under pressure to come off peat can do it without changing how they grow.
  • Ships compressed, so the freight is not spent carrying air and water.

What it is not

  • Not a fertiliser. It is what the roots sit in, not what feeds them.
  • Not all the same. Washing, screening and moisture decide how a given batch performs.
  • Not instant. It has to be wetted and allowed to open before it goes into a pot.

02 How it is used

Where it ends up, and what it is doing there.

Glasshouse fruit

Slabs and filled bags under drip irrigation, for tomato, pepper, cucumber and soft fruit grown on a long crop.

Propagation

Cubes and plugs for striking cuttings and raising young plants, where an even, low-salt material matters most.

Nursery containers

Blended into potting mixes to open up the structure and hold water between irrigations.

Substrate manufacture

Bought loose or as blocks and blended to a maker's own recipe, often with chip, perlite or bark.

Retail packs

Briquettes and discs in printed sleeves, hydrated by the person who buys them.

From block to root zone

A block is a shipping format. It only becomes useful once it is wetted.

State 01 / as loaded

Dense, dry and stackable

This is the state that makes the freight arithmetic work: you are shipping it at a fraction of the volume it will occupy. It is also the state in which every quality decision has already been taken.

State 02 / water added

Breaking down under water

Add water and the block gives way from the outside in. How cleanly it does that, and how much water it takes, follows from the compression ratio and how wet the pith was at the press.

State 03 / ready to fill

Open, airy, holding water

This is what the grower is actually buying: an open structure that holds water and air together. What it expands to depends on the batch, which is why that figure belongs on an analysis rather than on a web page.

04 Where it goes wrong

Cocopeat fails quietly.

Three things decide whether a container is right, and not one of them can be seen in a photograph or judged from a price.

01

Salt

Pith leaves the yard carrying sodium and potassium from the husk. Too much of it and a young crop stalls in its first weeks. Washing is what takes it out, and how thoroughly a batch was washed is invisible once it has been pressed.

02

Screening

The proportion of fine pith to coarse chip sets how much air the root zone keeps once it is wet. Screened for the wrong crop, the same material either dries out too fast or holds water where the roots need air.

03

Moisture

How wet the pith was when it went into the press decides what a block weighs and how cleanly it opens later. It is also the easiest of the three to get wrong on a busy day.

None of that is exotic and none of it is secret. It is simply why the grade is settled before production rather than argued about after arrival, and why the figures that matter arrive on the analysis for the batch you actually bought.
